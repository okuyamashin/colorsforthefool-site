I′ · Native speakers

Counting first languages only

Native speakers (dark) against all speakers (light) for the site's languages, in millions. Ethnologue (2026). By first language, these 17 languages add up to about 3.62 billion people, roughly 44% of the world. The gap to the 82.1% by all speakers is people who learned the language as a second language.

First languageAll speakers, including second language

For English and French, native speakers are a quarter or less of all speakers; most learned them at school or work. Indonesian is about 30% native. Portuguese, Japanese, Vietnamese, Korean, Spanish and Italian are almost entirely native. Arabic is left out because the standard Arabic used on the site has no native speakers (the spoken varieties used at home total over 300 million).

Large first languages the site does not cover yet: Western Punjabi 90M, Marathi and Telugu 83M each, Tamil 79M. Filipino has about 33 million native speakers, Ukrainian about 32 million, Polish about 40 million, and Swahili about 4 million. All four are below the 50 million line of this list, so none is in the chart.

I″ · Visitors to Japan

First languages of visitors to Japan

The top 30 first languages of people visiting Japan. Gemini's estimate of 7 October 2026, a simple sum of each language's share within the visitor's home country. The figures are approximate ranges, and each bar is the middle of its range. Traditional Chinese here is Taiwanese Mandarin; Simplified Chinese is mainland Mandarin. Twenty-two of these thirty languages are on the site. Urdu, Polish and Swahili are not in this top 30. The middles of the languages on the site add up to 82.7% (76.4–88.9% if the ranges are added instead). Cantonese (6.5–7.5%) is counted on its own and is not in that share. Cantonese speakers usually read Traditional Chinese, so the Traditional Chinese pages largely reach them.

V · Where attention outruns content

Attention against content

Up: daily hours online. Across: share of websites. Both scales are logarithmic. The further up and left, the more time people spend online in that language compared with how much is written in it.

Arabic gets 2.3 billion hours a day online, yet only 0.6% of websites are in Arabic. Chinese, Indonesian, Portuguese and Spanish sit on the same side. New languages should come from this upper-left corner.

Hindi has 1.74 billion hours online and under 0.1% of websites. Bengali (610 million hours) and Filipino (530 million hours) are also under 0.1%, so all three sit on the 0.05% line at the left edge. Thai is 0.3% of websites and 510 million hours. Urdu is on the site, at 520 million hours a day, but it is not in this top 40 for web content, so it is not on the chart. Swahili is on the site, but it is not in this top 40 for web content and has no row in the time estimate, so it is not on the chart.

VI · Beyond the yardsticks

Languages the numbers miss

In the charts above, African languages appear only in the speaker count. Internet users, web content and time online all measure people who are already online and already reading in an existing language. Most people in Africa are counted as users of English, French or Arabic.

English and French reach mainly people who went through school or use them at work. The people this site wants to reach, those whom schooling did not reach, fall through that gap. So when choosing languages we also look at how many people read only that language and how fast it is growing. About one in five or six people in the world live in Africa, the youngest region, where both population and internet use are still growing.

Languages to add from Africa

Hausa~94M speakers
The common language of West Africa. Northern Nigeria, Niger, northern Ghana. Written in Latin script, though some read it in Arabic script (Ajami).

Some people speak a language without being used to reading it. Read-aloud audio, not only text, would help. Nigerian Pidgin (~120M) and Amharic (~60M) are the next candidates.
